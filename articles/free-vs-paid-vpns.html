Free VPNs vs. Paid VPNs: Where the Free Ones Make Their Money

By the Privacy Plain English Team · September 14, 2026 · 7 min read

"If you're not paying for the product, you are the product." You've heard the saying. With free VPNs it's not just a saying — it's the business plan, written down in their privacy policies. Let's follow the money.

Why Running a VPN Costs Real Money

Before judging free VPNs, understand what they're supposedly giving away. A VPN service has to:

  • Rent servers in dozens of countries. A competitive paid VPN operates hundreds or thousands of servers across 50–100+ countries. Each server is rented compute with premium bandwidth — data-center bandwidth for a popular location can cost hundreds of dollars per server per month.
  • Pay for bandwidth at scale. Every byte you send through the tunnel is bandwidth the VPN company buys. A single heavy user streaming video through a VPN can push through hundreds of gigabytes a month. Multiply by millions of users.
  • Employ engineers and support staff. Maintaining apps for Windows, macOS, iOS, Android, plus browser extensions and router support, plus 24/7 customer support — that's a real payroll.
  • Pay for audits and infrastructure. The independent no-logs audits and security reviews we tell you to look for cost tens of thousands of dollars each, and reputable providers commission them every year or two.

Put it together and a serious VPN operation burns through serious money every month. So when an app offers you all of that for free, the only sane question is: where does the money come from?

The 4 Ways Free VPNs Pay Their Bills

1. Selling your data (the big one)

Remember: a VPN shifts visibility of your browsing from your ISP to the VPN company. A trustworthy paid provider is paid not to look. A free provider with no subscription revenue has the same visibility — and a financial incentive to monetize it. Browsing histories, device identifiers, and app-usage data are sold to data brokers and advertisers. This isn't theoretical; it's disclosed in many free VPNs' own privacy policies if you read past the "we value your privacy" preamble on the homepage.

2. Injecting ads

Some free VPNs insert their own advertisements into your browsing — banner ads, pop-ups, or affiliate link swaps injected into the pages you visit. You're trading a slightly more private connection for a measurably worse and less trustworthy browsing experience.

3. Selling your bandwidth and device access

The most alarming model: your device becomes part of the product. The free VPN routes other people's traffic through your connection (a residential proxy network), selling access to businesses. Your IP address becomes the exit point for strangers' browsing — including, potentially, activity you'd never want associated with your name.

4. The upsell squeeze

The mildest model: a deliberately crippled free tier — 500MB to 2GB of data a month, a handful of slow servers, one device — designed to frustrate you into upgrading. This one is at least honest about its mechanics. Whether the underlying company is trustworthy is a separate question.

Times Free VPNs Burned Their Users

These are documented, reported cases — not rumors:

  • Hola (2015). The popular free "VPN" was revealed to be selling users' bandwidth through its Luminati (now Bright Data) sister company — users' devices were used as exit nodes for paying customers' traffic without clear consent. Hola's founder confirmed the arrangement.
  • Onavo Protect (2018). Facebook's free VPN app, marketed as a privacy tool, was used to collect detailed app-usage data from users' phones to inform Facebook's competitive strategy. Apple pulled it from the App Store for violating data-collection rules; Facebook shut it down in 2019.
  • UFO VPN (2020). A free VPN claiming a strict no-logs policy was found with an exposed database containing over 20 million user logs — IP addresses, connection timestamps, and more. The "no logs" claim was fiction.
  • SuperVPN and GeckoVPN (2020). Two free VPN apps with over 100 million combined installs exposed 360+ million user records — including email addresses, device details, and payment information — in an unsecured database.

The pattern is consistent: when the user isn't the customer, the user's data is the inventory.

The Freemium Exception: When a Free Tier Is Legit

There is one kind of free VPN we don't object to: a limited free tier from a reputable paid provider. The two standouts: Proton VPN's free plan — unlimited data (rare for a free tier), but limited to a handful of server locations at slower speeds — and Windscribe's free tier — more server locations but capped at 10GB/month. Both are funded transparently as a funnel toward the paid product.

The business model is honest: they hope you like it and upgrade. The company has subscription revenue, published audits, and a reputation to protect. The free tier is limited enough that it can't be abused at scale, and the privacy policy is the same one paying customers get.

How to tell the difference: a legitimate freemium tier comes from a company that also sells a paid plan, publishes independent audits, and states its limits plainly. A sketchy free-only VPN has no visible revenue, vague ownership, and a privacy policy that permits "sharing data with partners."

The Math: What a Paid VPN Actually Costs Per Month

Here's the honest price landscape for reputable paid VPNs (prices drift with promotions, so treat these as ranges):

  • Monthly billing: roughly $10–13/month. Convenient, terrible value — only makes sense if you need a VPN for a single trip.
  • 1-year plans: roughly $4–6/month billed annually ($48–72/year).
  • 2-year plans: roughly $2–4/month billed upfront ($48–96 for two years). This is where the value lives, and it's what most committed users buy.

So the real cost of a trustworthy VPN, bought sensibly, is a few dollars a month — less than one streaming subscription. That's the entire price of moving your trust from an ad-supported free app to a company whose business model is your subscription.

And note the alignment: a paid provider's incentive is to keep you subscribed, which means not betraying you. A free provider's incentive is to extract value from you some other way. Follow the money, and the choice makes itself.

Bottom Line: Who Should Pay and Who Can Use Free

  • Pay if you use a VPN regularly — travel, remote work, daily privacy. A 1–2 year plan from an audited provider is one of the cheapest privacy upgrades that exists.
  • Use a legitimate freemium tier (Proton VPN, Windscribe) if you need a VPN rarely — one trip a year, occasional public Wi-Fi — and can live within the data cap. It's the only free option we'd touch.
  • Avoid free-only VPNs with no paid plan, no audits, and vague ownership. The documented history of this category is ugly, and you're handing the company the keys to your browsing.

FAQ

Are all free VPNs dangerous?
No — but the category "free-only VPN with no visible revenue" has a terrible track record. Freemium tiers from reputable paid providers are the exception, and they're limited on purpose.
Why is monthly billing so much more expensive?
It's deliberate pricing psychology: the monthly rate anchors high so the 1–2 year plans look like a deal. If you only need a VPN for one trip, one month at ~$11 is still fine — just cancel after.
Do paid VPNs ever have real discounts?
Constantly — and that's the catch. "83% off!" usually means 83% off an inflated monthly anchor price, not off a fair price. Compare the actual dollars-per-month on the 1–2 year plan, not the discount percentage.
Can I share one subscription with family?
Most paid plans allow 5–10 simultaneous device connections, which comfortably covers a household. Check the provider's device limit before buying — it's listed on every pricing page.
